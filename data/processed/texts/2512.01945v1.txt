ABSTRACT
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has advanced the reason-
ing capability of large language models (LLMs), enabling autonomous agents that
can conduct effective multi-turn and tool-integrated reasoning. While instructions
serve as the primary protocol for defining agents, RLVR typically relies on static
and manually designed instructions. However, those instructions may be subop-
timal for the base model, and the optimal instruction may change as the agent’s
policy improves and explores the interaction with the environment. To bridge the
gap, we introduce INSPO, a novelInstruction-policy Co-Evolution framework that
integrates instruction optimization as a dynamic component of the reinforcement
learning (RL) loop. INSPOmaintains a dynamic population of instruction can-
didates that are sampled with questions, where reward signals in RL loops are
automatically attributed to each instruction, and low performers are periodically
pruned. New instructions are generated and verified through an on-policy reflection
mechanism, where an LLM-based optimizer analyzes past experience from a replay
buffer and evolves more effective strategies given the current policy. We conduct
extensive experiments on multi-turn retrieval and reasoning tasks, demonstrating
that INSPOsubstantially outperforms strong baselines relying on static instructions.
INSPOdiscovers innovative instructions that guide the agent toward more strategic
reasoning paths, achieving substantial performance gains with only a marginal
increase in computational overhead.
1 INTRODUCTION
The advent of large language models (LLMs) (Brown et al., 2020; Chung et al., 2024) has given
rise to autonomous agents that are capable of reasoning, interpreting user intents, and tackling
complex tasks via interacting with the environment (Yao et al., 2023). When paired with carefully
engineered instructions, LLM-based agents have excelled in a wide range of applications, such as
code generation (Jimenez et al., 2023), retrieval-augmented generation (Trivedi et al., 2023), and
interactive decision-making (Su et al., 2025). Recently, the reinforcement learning (RL) (Sutton
et al., 1999) paradigm has further advanced the reasoning capabilities of LLM agents, enabling them
to learn policies from verifiable rewards (Shao et al., 2024) (RLVR) and achieve multi-turn and
tool-integrated reasoning (Jin et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025).
In the core of these agentic capabilities,instructionsserve as the protocol for programming these
agents, characterizing their roles, and defining any available tools/interfaces for interaction. The
performance of LLM-based agents has been shown to be highly dependent on the instruction (Zhou
et al., 2025), and subtle changes can exert substantial differences in generated trajectories, preventing
robust and generalizable agent applications. The compounding effect of instructions is further
amplified when LLMs are post-trained via RL, where changes in instructions can result in different
initial spaces for policy learning, thereby largely affecting the converged performance after training
(Liu et al., 2025a). Consequently, instruction design becomes crucial for agent training and typically
requires costly human efforts for iterative refinements via trial-and-error.

The traditional paradigm of RLVR treats instruction as astaticandpre-definedinput. However,
the optimal instruction for the base model is not always knowna prioriand may even change
as the model’s policy improves and explores the interaction with the environment (Soylu et al.,
2024). Recent findings also underscore the importance of instruction for RL, where injecting reward
specification (Zhang et al., 2025) or in-context hints (Liu et al., 2025b) into the instruction better
aligns the model with the learning objective and generates richer reward signals. While automated
prompt optimization (APO) (Zhou et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024) approaches exist for obtaining a
better instruction before commencing the RL phase, generalizing them to the online setting of RL
and incorporating adaptive knowledge during policy updates is rather non-trivial.
To bridge this gap, we propose to automate instruction learning not as a static term, but as an
integral and dynamic component of the RL learning loop, allowing the instruction and policy to
co-evolve in an online setup. We introduce INSPO, INStruction-POlicy co-evolution, for agentic policy
optimization, a novel framework that delivers two major innovations:
• Policy-Optimization with Dynamic Instructions: In contrast to existing RLVR approaches that
rely on a static instruction, INSPOmaintains a dynamic population of instruction candidates.
During policy optimization, instructions are sampled from this population and weighted by
their importance. The reward signals not only provide the policy gradients, but also attribute
reward signals to each instruction candidate based on the assignment. We periodically prune low-
performing candidates via successive halving and evolve new instructions from high-performing
ones, ensuring a self-improving set of instructions that learns to incorporate new knowledge for
policy learning.
• Experience-Driven Instruction Generation: To generate innovative and effective instruction
candidates, we introduce an on-policy reflection mechanism that mimics the idea of experience
replay. INSPOutilizes a replay buffer that prioritizes past trajectories where the agent made
incorrect or low-reward predictions. The history of experience then provides a reflection signal to
an LLM-based instruction-proposer module, which analyzes the failure cases and evolves new
instructions via self-reflection. Lastly, the newly proposed instructions are verified via a low-cost
proxy before merging into the active population, where low-performing candidates are rejected.
We validate our approach through extensive experiments on multi-turn retrieval and reasoning tasks.
The experimental results show that INSPOsubstantially outperforms strong baselines that use static
instructions. We demonstrate that INSPOevolves precise and innovative instructions that guide the
LLM to discover more strategic reasoning paths and avoid erroneous patterns. Our ablation study
further confirms that the online co-optimization of instruction and policy outperforms optimizing
the instruction either before or after the RL phase. By enabling the online automation of instruction
optimization alongside the policy optimization, INSPOincurs only a marginal computation overhead
to the RL training process while bringing substantial performance gains.

2.1 REINFORCEMENTLEARNING WITHMULTI-TURNTOOL-USING
We formalize multi-turn tool-using as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) (Hauskrecht et al., 2013),
where the LLM-based agent, governed by its policy π and parameterized by the weights θ, interacts
with an external environment by calling a tool engine T . It generates a trajectory τ as its state
that comprises multiple turns of interactions: τ= (I, q, a0, o0, . . . , ak−1, ok−1, ak), where I is the
initial instruction and q is the question input. ak is the response from the LLM given the history
trajectory up to turn k, expressing the action of the agent for each turn, such that a∼π θ(·|I, q;T).
The observation o is the feedback from the environment by calling the tool engine, ok =T(a k),
which is concatenated in an interleaved manner with the action that forms the final trajectory.
The learning objective of RL is to optimize the policy parameters θ by maximizing the expected
returns over a distribution of questions D, where r(τ) defines the reward function. In RLVR,
the reward is typically defined via a rule-based verifier, such as an exact match reward function,
rEM(τ)∈ {1,0}, which returns 1 only if the final action matches the ground truth, and 0 otherwise.
Therefore, the learning objective of RL is expressed as:

3 INSTRUCTION-POLICYCO-EVOLUTION(INSPO)
The conventional RLVR approach relies on a static and pre-defined instruction, which may be
suboptimal and fails to adapt to observations from the environment as the agent’s policy updates,

potentially constraining the exploration of the agent and resulting in a local optimum for policy
learning. Instead, INSPOre-frames the instruction design in RLVR not as a one-off human engineering,
but as a dynamic and integral component of the RL loop that can be automatically optimized.
We introduce INSPO,Instruction-policy co-evolution, a novel co-evolving framework where the
instruction is optimized alongside the agent’s policy learning, enabling the discovery of more
effective strategies and reasoning pathways in an online setting. The core of INSPOis composed
of two synergistic components: policy optimization with a dynamic set of instructions and an
experience-driven self-reflection mechanism for generating new instructions based on the current
policy.

To ensure a self-improving population of instructions, the population P is periodically pruned and

Halving algorithm (Jamieson & Talwalkar, 2016), where the bottom half of instruction candidates,

parents to evolve new and potentially superior instructions.

tuple of (I, q, τ, r(τ))into the buffer B. We prioritize storing trajectories that resulted in incorrect
or low-reward predictions from the latest step, creating a source of correction signals based on past
failures. Periodically, we evolve new instruction candidates by this on-policy reflection mechanism
that involves three key steps:
1. Select: When the early phase has pruned the population to a target size, we select the top Nparent
instructions as the best-performing candidates to serve as parents for evolution. We randomly
sample one instructionI t at a time to form the parent for the generation process.
2. Generate: Together with the parent instruction, we sample a batch of failure trajectories from the
replay buffer B that were generated in the latest step. These trajectories contain rich information
that reveals the failure modes of the newest policy and its history of interaction with the environ-
ment. We then leverage an LLM-based optimizer O to reflect on failure patterns and propose new
instruction candidates. Precisely, the optimizer is prompted with the parent instruction and the
failure trajectories, and is tasked with performing self-reflection: it analyzes the causes of failures,
critiques the existing strategy in instruction, and proposes refined instructions {Inew} designed to
guide the agent to overcome these erroneous actions. This stands as a critical step for INSPOthat
constantly involves online feedback into the policy learning process.
3. Verify:Newly generated instructions are finally verified before merging into the active population
to maintain the stability of the training. Each proposed candidate Inew is evaluated via a low-cost
proxy, where we sample a subset of samples DB from the replay buffer for validation, and only
the top-performing candidates are added to the new populationP t.
This generate-and-verify loop continues until the population is refilled to the target population size
NP, ensuring a self-improving population of instructions. In the inference time, we only return the
best instruction with the highest importance weight I∗ =argmax Ij∈PT wj, which is portable and
transferable for LLM inference. The complete procedure for INSPOis provided in Algorithm 1.
Overall, the dynamic instruction sampling integrates natively with the policy optimization loop for
exploring diverse reasoning strategies, and the experience-driven self-reflection mechanism enables
the agent to incorporate new environmental feedback into the policy learning process.

4 EXPERIMENTS
Models and evaluation data.We evaluate our approach on a diverse set of tool-integrated question-
answering benchmarks with the search tool. For multi-hop question answering, which requires
complex reasoning and multi-turn information retrieval, we include HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018),
2WikiMQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023). For
general question answering, we include Natural Questions (NQ) (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), TriviaQA
(Joshi et al., 2017), and PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023). We conduct INSPOexperiments on Qwen
2.5 series (Qwen et al., 2025), specifically the 3B and 7B versions. We leverage the state-of-the-art
reasoning model, Gemini 2.5 Pro (Comanici et al., 2025), as the LLM-based optimizer.
Baselines.We compare INSPOagainst the following baselines that all come with a static instruction:
• Model Inference: We include baselines without tool-using. We compare against the direct
inference approach (Direct), Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) (Chung et al., 2024), and GRPO
(Shao et al., 2024) on the same question-answering training data mixture without tool integration.
• Tool-Integrated Methods: For tool-integrated reasoning, we include Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020), IRCoT (Trivedi et al., 2023), Search-o1 (Li et al., 2025a),
SFT (reject sampling) (Jin et al., 2025), and the state-of-the-art Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025)
approach that extends GRPO to multi-turn interleaved reasoning with a search engine.
Setups.For all tool-integrated experiments, we follow the experimental setup as Search-R1, and use
the E5 (Wang et al., 2022) as the retriever with a knowledge source on the 2018 Wikipedia dump
(Karpukhin et al., 2020). The training data consists of a mixture of the NQ and HotpotQA training
sets. We use the Exact Match (EM) as the reward function. For all inference-only baselines, we
report the performance from instruct models, and the rest using the base models for training.
In the INSPOframework, we maintain a population size NP of 7, Nparent of 2 for exploration, and
a temperature τs of 0.2 in computing the selection probability for balancing the exploration and
exploitation on candidates. We set a pruning period Kp at 5 and the evolution period Ke at 15. We
evolve Te = 150 steps out of a total of 300 steps, thereby allowing the policy to be sufficiently
optimized with the INSPO-evolved instruction. We refer readers to App. §B for more details.
5 RESULTS
Main results.We present the main results of INSPOcompared to baselines in Table 1. INSPOyields
substantial performance improvements over model inference and tool-integrated baselines that all
come with a static instruction. INSPOachieves an average EM score of 38.2% on all benchmarks
with Qwen-2.5-3B, surpassing the state-of-the-art RL baseline with the search tool, Search-R1, by
a large margin of 6%. This performance advantage is consistent when scaling to the 7B model. In
particular, we observe that INSPOexcelled in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks, such as HotpotQA
and 2WikiMQA, where INSPOoutperforms the Search-R1 baseline over 7% in the 3B model. This
is further reflected in Fig. 2 (b), where the co-evolution of instruction and policy enables the agent
to execute a greater number of valid tool calls, thereby leveraging more turns of interactions with
the environment to resolve challenging questions. In contrast, the baseline, constrained by a static
instruction, converges to a policy with a mean of tool calls that is marginally above 1, limiting its
ability to decompose and solve multi-hop questions. In addition, we observe that INSPOperiodically
evolves to longer prompt length as shown in Fig. 2 (c). It validates the functionality of the self-
reflection mechanism in instruction generation, where INSPOconstantly involves environmental
feedback to refine the instruction with new details. These results collectively show that by treating
the instruction as a dynamic and optimizable component of the learning loop, INSPOunlocks more
effective and strategic tool-integrated reasoning paths.

Instruction Evolution Stage.We compare our online co-evolution with two offline APO alternatives:
pre-policy optimization, where the instruction is optimizedbeforeRL training begins, and post-policy
optimization, where the instruction is tunedafterthe training is complete. We observe that only
the pre-policy brings subtle gains to the converged performance, whereas the post-policy model has
become overfitted to the initial instruction and fails to adapt. It validates that the optimal instruction
changes as the policy learns, and co-evolution is crucial for achieving the best performance.
Instruction Proposer.We replace our reflection-based optimizer with two simpler APO alternatives
to ablate the effectiveness of the reflection mechanism. In Table 2, we include a paraphrasing-based
optimizer (Zhou et al., 2023) that generates I∼ O(·|Iold) and a history-based (Yang et al., 2024)
optimizer with I∼ O(·|{Ii, ri}n
i=1) that conditions on instruction-reward pairs. It shows that both
optimizers are inadequate to yield gains along the policy optimization. It underscores the importance
of the self-reflection mechanism that incorporates online feedback to generate instructions.
Instruction Population.Finally, we ablate the core modules in INSPO. We start from the INSPOwith
a constant population size without pruning, which shows an average score of 33.0%. Applying
the periodic pruning of low-performing candidates (+ Pruning) further improves performance to
36.3%, and the full INSPOapproach that includes the generate-verify loop (+ Verify) achieves the best
performance. This highlights the importance of maintaining a high-quality population of instructions,
which can drive the policy models towards better policy and generate richer reward signals.
6 RELATEDWORK
Reinforcement Learning for LLMs.Reinforcement learning (RL) (Sutton et al., 1999) has been
a powerful paradigm for post-training LLMs, notably in aligning LLMs with human preference
(Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023). It typically trains a reward model and optimizes the
policy via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017). Recently, DeepSeek-R1
(Guo et al., 2025) has demonstrated the effectiveness of using rule-based verifiable rewards (RLVR)
in advancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, where GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) obviates the need
for a critic model by group-wise sampling. DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) further introduces techniques that
improve the training stabilities of RLVR, such as clipping-higher and dynamic sampling. In addition,
Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025a) rectifies the length bias in GRPO. Orthogonal to these approaches,
INSPOserves as a modular component to the typical RLVR learning loop.

Tool-Integrated Agents.LLMs can be instructed to follow the pattern of reasoning and acting, which
enables agentic applications with tool integration (Yao et al., 2023). For instance, IRCoT (Trivedi
et al., 2023) interleaved chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022) with information retrieval.
Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023) trains LLMs with diverse tool usages via SFT. More recently, the
RL paradigm has led to agents with multi-turn tool interactions (Feng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b),
where the search tool has excelled in resolving question-answering tasks (Jin et al., 2025; Song et al.,
2025). Though there is a growth of end-to-end RL frameworks for multi-turn tool-use (Xue et al.,
2025; Jiang et al., 2025), the importance of instruction design for agentic policy optimization has
been largely overlooked, and they all rely on a costly manual design for the instruction that initializes
the interaction with tools while treating it as a static component along the learning process.
Instruction Optimization.The instruction serves as the protocol for defining the agents’ behaviors,
where language agents have been exposed to sensitivity with respect to prompt designs (Zhou
et al., 2025). Traditional instruction optimization (IO) approaches rely on simply paraphrasing
the instruction (Zhou et al., 2023; 2024). Wan et al. (2024; 2025) emphasizes the importance of
demonstration/exemplars together with the general instruction design. Recent approaches integrate
LLM-based optimizers (Yang et al., 2024) and leverage textual gradients (Pryzant et al., 2023;
Y¨uksekg¨on¨ul et al., 2025) to optimize the instruction, where Agrawal et al. (2025) show that reflective
prompt design can even outperform the RL paradigm. Previously, Soylu et al. (2024) propose
interleaving IO with SFT. In the pioneering context of RL, different from traditional IO approaches
that optimize the instruction before commencing the RL training (Zhang et al., 2025), INSPOis
the first framework to integrate IO directly into the online RL loop, co-evolving instructions with
on-policy experience and environmental feedback.
7 CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced INSPO, a novel framework that enables the co-evolution of instructions
and policy learning for agentic policy optimization. Different to existing RLVR approaches that
rely on static, manually-designed instructions, INSPOmaintains a dynamic population of instruction
candidates and employs an experience-driven instruction generation mechanism, automating the
discovery of more effective reasoning strategies via reflecting on online feedback along the policy
learning process. Our experiments demonstrate that INSPOsubstantially outperforms strong baselines
with static instructions on a wide range of tool-integrated question-answering benchmarks. The
case studies and ablation analyses further confirmed that the online, reflection-based co-evolution of
instructions is key to guiding the agent toward more robust and accurate tool-integrated reasoning.
INSPOrepresents a significant step toward creating a more autonomous and adaptive RL framework,
reducing the burden of manual prompt engineering and unlocking new capabilities in LLM agents.

A FUTUREWORK ANDLIMITATIONS
While INSPOdemonstrates substantial performance improvement, the effectiveness of our experience-
driven instruction generation relies on a capable LLM-based optimizer for self-reflection and generat-
ing sophisticated instructions. We have primarily studied using Gemini 2.5 Pro as the optimizer, and
we will endeavor to explore other thinking-style models as part of our future work. We would like
to note that INSPOstill introduces a marginal but non-zero computation overhead that includes the
API costs for the LLM-based optimizer and the inference cost for the phase of verification, which we
have shown is marginal compared to the cost of the total training. Lastly, our current experiments
have primarily focused on search-based question answering tasks. We believe the core principles of
INSPOare broadly applicable to other agentic domains, and we will explore extending the framework
with other available tools in future work.
B IMPLEMENTATIONDETAILS
We build our codebase on the VeRL framework (Sheng et al., 2025). Our experimental setup,
including the training and evaluation data mixture, follows that of Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025). We
conduct our training on a node of 8×H100 GPUs. For all retrieval-based methods, we keep the top-3
retrieved passages from the retriever. We used a learning rate of 5e-7 for theQwen2.5-3B-base
model and 3e-7 for Qwen2.5-7B-base model. All methods were trained for a total of 300 steps
with a cosine learning rate schedule with a warm-up ratio of 0.1, which are then evaluated every 100
steps, and we report the final checkpoint before any reward collapse.
For both GRPO and INSPOtraining. We set the group size G to 5. We used a batch size of 512, a
mini-batch size of 256, and a micro-batch size of 64. We set the maximum prompt length to 4096
and truncate response and observation lengths to 500 for each turn. The maximum number of turns
