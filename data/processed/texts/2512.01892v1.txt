With the rapid uptake of generative AI, investigating human perceptions of generated responses has become crucial. A major challenge
is their ‘aptitude’ for hallucinating and generating harmful contents. Despite major efforts for implementing guardrails, human
perceptions of these mitigation strategies are largely unknown. We conducted a mixed-method experiment for evaluating the responses
of a mitigation strategy across multiple-dimensions: faithfulness, fairness, harm-removal capacity, and relevance. In a within-subject
study design, 57 participants assessed the responses under two conditions: harmful response plus its mitigation and solely mitigated
response. Results revealed that participants’ native language, AI work experience, and annotation familiarity significantly influenced
evaluations. Participants showed high sensitivity to linguistic and contextual attributes, penalizing minor grammar errors while
rewarding preserved semantic contexts. This contrasts with how language is often treated in the quantitative evaluation of LLMs. We
also introduced new metrics for training and evaluating mitigation strategies and insights for human-AI evaluation studies.

1 Introduction
As generative AI systems become increasingly integrated into decision-making and communication platforms, ensuring
their outputs are safe, fair, and contextually appropriate is critical. Generative AI systems may generate sentences with
hallucinations [20, 22], produce offensive content [52]; and hiding strategies not aligned to human expectations [14, 29].
Model-related mitigation techniques have being created recently to assure the detection of harms [35], using adversarial
training and [10]. Those approaches brought significant advances to mitigate LLM outputs [3, 27, 34, 44, 47, 50, 54] and
additional challenges emerged to evaluate the real representation and quality of data being generated. To evaluate, at
scale, the massive amount of data is challenging. Therefore, new approaches for making feasible human evaluation are
necessary in order to inform the development of the mitigation techniques.
This study investigates how humans perceive and evaluate AI-generated responses that have been modified by
a mitigator model to reduce harm. We explore three key dimensions of risk mitigation in AI-generated responses:
mitigation performance, transparency, and metrics. Our research questions are as follows:
Evaluating the mitigation performance
•RQ1:To what extent did the participants choose the mitigated response over the unmitigated response? Why?
In which cases?
•RQ2:How relevant and fair did participants find the mitigated responses?
•RQ3:Does the mitigation approach effectively reduce harm while preserving the core message of the original
(unmitigated) response?
Evaluating the transparency of mitigation
•RQ4:Did exposure to the unmitigated response influence how participants evaluated the mitigated version?
Informing the mitigator development process
•RQ5:What metrics — both established and newly proposed by participants — are suitable for evaluating the
quality and effectiveness of risk mitigation models?
Our findings contribute to narrowing the socio-technical gap in AI evaluation and propose new metrics for assessing
risk mitigation in generative AI.
2 Related work
In this section, we discuss themes and previous works that motivated the study we present in this paper, namely, the
advancements and gaps in the literature pertaining to social value alignment, AI risks, and human-evaluation of LLM.
2.1 Social value alignment
Social value alignment is the process of training AI to be operational according to a set of values that are important
for humans. However, given that the world has pluralistic views, concepts about values varies culturally, timely and
personally to list a few. Therefore, the decision process of which values are embedded into AI models and those who
decides these values are controversial aspects [14].
In technical terms, there are many methods used to align AI models, such as the ones based on Supervised Fine-Tuning
(SFT) [44, 47, 54], Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [38], and Reinforcement Learning-based (with human or AI

Whether using values defined by preference lists annotated by humans or automatically defined by models using

One attempt to define criteria that align values almost to a level of user personalization is the work of [41], in which
agents mimic human actions in an environment that encodes moral context and challenges the user to keep its moral
choices even in difficult situations. Similarly, [13] works on the idea of user-driven alignment, in which humans engage
directly with AI systems to correct behaviors and guide the AI toward reflecting their values and ethical standards
throughout real-world interactions. Also understanding that social values are heterogeneous, the work of [53] develop
alignment strategies that are oriented to particular groups while preserving the “HHH’ criteria. For example, nurses
and doctors have altruistic values that should work to prioritize patients.
Despite the technical advancements, independently if they are based on universal or personalized criteria, we
understand that evaluate how society reacts to these instances of social-value alignment needs to be addressed
appropriately, what the current literature may be missing due to interpretation of AI risks and evaluation, as seen in
the upcoming sections.
2.2 AI risks and correction criteria for evaluating LLM
According to Weidingger [48], evaluating risks from AI systems is a priority for companies [1, 32], public policy makers
and regulators[12], and civil society [11].
Although it is a central concern to AI safety, thus far, there is no general conceptualization that researchers, developers
and general public can rely on. Instead, there are a myriad of works with their own organization criteria for defining and
list AI risks. As mentioned above, taxonomies is one way of organizing and conceptualize risks [48, 51], with authors
focusing on LLMs and Generative AI [42], while others address AI-based technologies in general [49].
Rao et al. (2025) [39] uses the definition from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)
[33], which defines risk as the chance of harm, and harm as a risk that has caused damage. However, they state that
studies about how developers describe model risks (or harms) and how they fail in typical scenarios are under explored
in the current literature. To address this issue, in one of their studies, Rao et al. (2025) [39] analyzed model cards from
models available on HuggingFace [21], and found out that developers tend to report risks related to technical aspects
such as limitation and bias, while researchers report risks related to governance, societal impact, and threats to human
agency. However, malicious use and misiformation, which are the largest cause of incidents as reported by The AI
Incident Database [30], are misrepresented by both developers and researchers reports.
As we show in the following section (Human-evaluation of LLM), not only developers, designers and researchers
have different views when describing AI risks. It also happens among developers and users when to decide to what pay
attention when evaluating models.
As we demonstrate in the following section, differing perspectives on AI risks are not limited to developers, designers,
and researchers. These differences also emerge between creators and end-users, particularly when deciding which
criteria of model behavior deserve attention during developing and evaluation. More than that, throughout this whole

2.3 Human-evaluation of LLM
It is expected that AI systems will be largely adopted in a myriad of areas, such as medical treatment [6], game playing
[16], advertisement [45], and autonomous vehicles [18].
However, AI systems are also target of critics, specifically tackling issues related to human-value alignment and
responsible practices (or lack of it) in their development and usage [14]. Problems from this nature may harm public
perception about AI-based technology and negatively impact their cycle of adoption as pointed by the results from the
British Science Association [7] and studies, such as the one from Weidinger et al. 2003 [48] as stated in the previous
subsection (AI risks and correction criteria for evaluating LLM).
Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate AI systems from the human perspective to detect and treat problems, such as
value alignment, early on. The work from Oh et al. (2017) [31] analyzed human perceptions during the demonstration of
AlphaGo [46]. The authors revealed that issues such as preexisting stereotypes and prejudices about AI, combined with
anthropomorphism and alienation created a scenario of technophobia, shocking reactions and apprehension. More over,
the work from [5] evaluated users expectations about trustworthy LLM standards. The results identify that users tend to
assess the fulfillment of standards - such as security, safety and fairness - according to how transparent the information
is, and how the data handling process happened. Unfortunately, according to the authors, there is a mismatch between
what users understand by LLM safety standards and those who defines the standards during model production.
This mismatch between model creators (or regulators) and users is another motivation for evaluating AI systems
according to human expectations. Currently, there are many works whose content evaluation is automatized by
algorithms and do not require human participation, as seen in the study from Zhang et al. (2025) [ 53]. Although
automatic evaluation are useful for technical aspects such as benchmarking models and verifying their training
results, it does little (or nothing) for measuring human reactions, specially in this context of pluralistic views about
value-alignment [14] and what constitutes AI risks [33, 39, 48].
2.4 Evaluation Criteria
The selection of evaluation criteria is a critical methodological decision that shapes how systems are assessed and
interpreted. Prior work has highlighted the diversity of evaluation strategies across domains. For instance, Ledo et
al. [26] identified four dominant strategies in HCI toolkit research—demonstration, usage, technical benchmarks,
and heuristic evaluation—emphasizing the need to align evaluation methods with research claims. Recent tools like
MetricMate[ 15] have begun to address this gap by supporting the generation of evaluation criteria for LLM-as-a-judge
workflows. MetricMate provides structured guidance for defining metrics aligned with task goals and user expectations,
underscoring the growing need for interactive, context-aware evaluation design.
Building on these insights, our approach contributes a stakeholder-centered methodology for defining evaluation
criteria in the context of socio-technical systems. Unlike prior work that often focuses on technical or usability metrics
in isolation, our method integrates domain expertise, user expectations, and ethical considerations from the outset.
This aligns with recent calls for more human-centered and ethically grounded AI evaluation practices, such as those
proposed by Rismani and Moon [40], who outline the roles and skills of responsible AI practitioners, and Liao and
Vaughan [28], who advocate for transparency and stakeholder engagement in the age of large language models.

3 Description of the mitigator model
This paper is part of a broader research initiative aimed at addressing the mitigation challenge through a novel post-hoc
methodology that decouples risk mitigation from the primary response generation process of large language models
(LLMs). Our approach involves developing small, modular language models that function as specializedmitigators,
capable of aligning LLM outputs to predefined safety criteria while minimizing computational overhead and preserving
model performance. Themitigatormodels are trained on synthetically generated structured datasets, where each data
instance is systematically annotated to indicate the presence or absence of harmful or biased content. The training
methodology employs in-context learning (ICL), wherein models learn to generate bias-free responses by leveraging
the structured exemplars provided during training. During inference, themitigatorreceives three inputs: the original
user prompt, the base model’s potentially problematic response, and a specification of the target risk category to be
addressed. The mitigator subsequently generates a refined response with appropriately mitigated risks, encompassing
categories such as social bias, violent content, and profanity. A critical requirement for the success of this framework is
the development of robust mechanisms for synthesizing training data with those specific requirements for effective
mitigator training.
4 Methodology
This section outlines the experimental design, metric selection, and data sampling strategies employed in our study.
While the primary contributions lie in the results and analysis, the methodology reflects a rigorous and collaborative
approach to ensure the reliability and relevance of the findings.
We collaborated with a multidisciplinary team—including experts in machine learning, ethics, and business strategy
to chose the main criteria to include in the evaluation studies. This process involved a structured workshop with seven
participants (three HCI researchers, two project executives, and two software developers), aimed at: Defining and
refining personas relevant to the mitigator project, Surfacing stakeholder expectations and mental models, Informing
the design of the evaluation platform and recruitment strategy.
This collaborative process resulted in the definition of three personas: End-users, mitigator Developers, and Project
Leaders. These personas informed the selection of evaluation criteria. A 60-minute virtual workshop, facilitated by an
HCI research scientist, guided participants through structured activities. Participants were divided into breakout rooms,

the harms present in the original response.
•Business Utility: Practical value of outputs in real-world decision-making contexts.

operational effectiveness.

4.1 Choosing the data sample
The dataset used in our experiments was synthetically generated using large language models (LLMs), seeded with
human-authored prompts for in context-learning to ensure contextual richness and thematic diversity. These prompts
were carefully designed to span a broad spectrum of socially and culturally sensitive topics, including:Health habits,
Dress codes,Patriotism,Gender identity,Moral values. To maintain thematic coherence and minimize redundancy, we
employed cosine similarity measures to validate prompt diversity. This validation was benchmarked against the Quora
Question Pairs dataset, enabling us to identify and filter semantically similar prompts. The resulting dataset offers
a controlled yet diverse sample, supporting robust evaluation across multiple dimensions of model behavior. This
approach ensures that the evaluation is grounded in realistic and ethically relevant scenarios.

4.3 Study protocol
The study was conducted in two stages, designed to evaluate the effectiveness and perception of mitigated responses in
human-computer interaction.
•Stage 1: Blind Evaluation of Mitigated Responses. Participants were presented with mitigated responses generated
by the system. They were not shown the original responses. Each participant rated the mitigated responses based
on two criteria: Fairness and Relevance. In this stage, each participant saw 6 examples. 3 in the Fairness criteria
and 3 in the Relevance criteria counterbalance by order in each metric to reduce effects and improve internal
validity [37].
•Stage 2: Comparative Evaluation. Participants were then shown pairs of original and mitigated responses.
They were asked to compare and evaluate the differences, focusing on Fairness, Relevance, Competence and
Faithfulness. For Fairness and Relevance criteria, participants saw the same examples from Stage 1, we wanted to
compare if their score changed when seeing the Original answer and not only the Mitigated one. In this phase we
included the Competence and Faithfulness criteria to grasp awareness of mitigator model perceived performance.

and received a gift voucher redeemable in the company’s internal store as a token of appreciation.
4.4 Study platform
We developed an scalable cloud-based interactive evaluation for the study enabling participants interaction thought
their own browsers. We employed a within-subjects design [23] where each participant evaluated responses across two
phases: In Phase 1, participants assessed mitigated responses independently, evaluating fairness and relevance without
seeing the original model outputs; In Phase 2, participants compared original and mitigated responses side-by-side,
rating the mitigated responses across five dimensions: faithfulness, fairness, relevance, competence, and business risks.
Such a design enabled us to measure both standalone perceptions of mitigation quality and comparative assessments
relative to original outputs (see figures 1, 2).

4.5.1 Quantitative analysis.The quantitative analysis in this study employed a multi-method statistical approach
to evaluate participant responses and perceived model performance. Initially, descriptive analysis was conducted to
summarize and understand the general characteristics of the dataset, including participant demographics and response
distributions. The Chi-square test was applied to assess relationships between categorical variables, which is particularly
suitable for nominal data and allowed for the identification of statistically significant differences in response patterns.
Fisher’s exact test was used to validate findings for smaller sample sizes or when expected frequencies were low. To
further explore the strength of associations between variables, Cramér’s V was calculated, offering insight into the
degree of correlation among factors such as language, experience with AI, and response preferences. Finally, logistic
regression was employed to model the probability of participants choosing the mitigated response over the original.
This approach was done for individual variables and in combination, followed by an ANOVA based on Chi-square to
evaluate the overall model fit. This approach enabled us to identify the key predictors and patterns in our sample.
To make the score distribution analysis, we separated the data in 2 different groups Phase 1 and Phase 2. For
each group, we separated in subgroups according to the number of metrics each one had, 2 for Phase 1 (fairness and
relevance) and 4 for Phase 2 (fairness, relevance, faithfulness, competence). Finally, for each metric subgroup, we
separated in others subgroups according to the independent variables, specifically workplace allocation, first language,
AI annotation experience, and overall experience with AI. We aimed to investigate how the participants evaluate the
mitigator performance and if diversity has influence on this evaluation.
4.5.2 Qualitative analysis.In the survey, participants responded to open-ended questions explaining their rationale for
selecting either a mitigated or original answer, and provided additional reflections in response to the final question at
the end of the study. A researcher conducted initial coding of these responses. We analyzed participants’ rationales and
interview transcripts using a reflexive thematic analysis [9, 17]. Two researchers independently coded this qualitative
data, collaboratively discussed the emergent codes, and developed an initial version of a codebook to guide the analysis
[9, 17]. The corpus consisted of 1083 open-ended survey responses from 57 participants including 17 semi-structured
interview transcripts (mean length=60 minutes). Following open coding on a pilot set of 17 transcripts, the researchers
conducted four debriefing sessions throughout the interview process, typically after every four interviews. These
sessions served to refine the interview approach and identify emerging insights and codes, guided by questions such as:
“What value tensions are participants describing?”,“What constitutes a ‘good’ mitigation?”, and“Where is faithfulness
versus relevance being conflated?”. The final codebook contained four main parent themes and 16 sub-themes (as detailed
in Table 5). Any disagreements among the coders were resolved through iterative discussions, with a third researcher if
needed.
Following the completion of all interviews, the researchers revisited the debriefing notes, compared them against the
initial codebook, and incorporated new codes as necessary. Each transcript was then analyzed in depth, and codes were
iteratively grouped into higher-order themes through axial and theoretical coding, constantly compared against memos
and debrief notes. Analysis was concluded when no new explanatory insights emerged, indicating saturation. These
analyses resulted in the identification of the main themes detailed in Section 5.

RQ1 - To what extent did the participants choose the mitigated response over the unmitigated response?
Why? In what cases specifically?
We analyzed human preferences between mitigated and adversarial responses using a null model. On average, human
judges preferred mitigated responses 66.8% of the time, a statistically significant result ( 𝑝< 2 ×10−16), even when
independent variables were not considered. This suggests a strong baseline preference for mitigated responses in
adversarial contexts.
To explore the influence of demographic and experiential factors, we computed odds ratios across several groups.
Regarding location, individuals working outside the US were 20% less likely to choose mitigated responses compared to
those working in the US. Language background also showed variation: participants with South-Asian first languages
were 50% less likely, and those with Latin-based first languages were 30% less likely to prefer mitigated responses than
native English speakers. Interestingly, participants from the "Others" language group were 40% more likely to favor
mitigated responses. Experience with crowdsourcing and AI also impacted preferences. Participants without annotation
experience were 30% less likely to choose mitigated responses, while those with more than six months of AI experience
were 20% less likely to do so compared to those with less experience.
Despite these nuanced differences, our analysis found no statistically significant evidence that diversity factors,
such as, location, language, or experience—meaningfully influenced the overall preference for mitigated responses.
This suggests that while individual characteristics may shape response selection to some extent, the general human
preference for mitigation remains a dominant and consistent pattern across diverse populations.

suggest that exposure to the original response led participants to assign lower scores to the mitigator answer, rather
than higher ones.
To determine whether individual differences influenced the observed score changes, we performed both Chi-square
tests and Fisher’s exact tests on several independent variables. These included location, first language, annotation
experience, and AI experience. None of these variables showed statistically significant associations with score change
indicating the demographic and experiential factors did not significantly influence the likelihood of score change,
reinforcing the conclusion that the observed effect was primarily driven by the intervention (i.e., viewing the original
response).
Overall, participants consistently preferred the mitigated response over the original one across all condi-
tions. Their evaluation scores stayed mostly stable. However, when they saw the original response while
rating the mitigated version, they often gave lower scores. This might suggest that seeing the original made
them think more critically, leading to a more careful evaluation of the mitigated content.

6 Key insights from the human studies
Our research yields key insights from human studies that inform the design of generative models capable of mitigating
harms while maintaining fairness, relevance, and fidelity. These findings establish essential criteria for LLM mitigation
frameworks and clarify expected performance standards from a human perspective. The key insights are as following:
(1) Models should remove harms, political bias, moral bias, moral judgments and preserve the key information
answering the prompt avoiding hallucinations. Essential criteria: Relevance.
(2) Grammatical and Punctuation errors and lack of logical structure affected the way people evaluated Fairness, trust,
and quality of the response. Essential criteria: Coherence criteria is essential when synthetic data is generated
(SDG).
(3) Provide Objective and factual responses when given several arguments, several options, and not provide gen-
eralized and neutral answers. Essential criteria: Faithfulness metric and Factuality metric to preserve the key
information and real world representation outputs.
(4) Figurative language including sarcasm, emotions, opinions, human like recommendations, first person sentences,
and informal language - affected trust and were perceived as less fair by our participants. Essential criteria: Tone
should be included in SDG outputs.
(5) Should remove harms from the answer and preserve in the text what was not considered a risk. Should not
generate information that was not in the original answer. Changing the content of the response from the original
affected trust and quality of the mitigated response. Essential criteria: Selective Mitigation should be a criteria.
Inclusion of a score evaluation to avoid risk detection and activate the mitigator model when not necessary. Add
samples of outputs with harmful and not harmful sentences to help the mitigator in decision-making.
(6) Responses should be complete and self-sufficient. Essential criteria: Outputs should be complete and standalone.
(7) Trade offs metrics - Model evaluations should account for conflicting values and incorporate a scoring framework
that reflects these trade-offs. In our study, some participants, frequently found themselves balancing fairness
against other criteria like Relevance and Faithfulness, and often considered these criteria being competing or even
conflicting. This led them to sometimes favor original and less fair but more relevant responses over mitigated
outputs that addressed bias but diverged from addressing the prompt.

7 Discussion
In general, human participants showed greater preference for mitigated responses. This is expected given the nature of
the technology being evaluated, namely, the mitigation technique, designed to reduce the levels of content harmfulness.
Nonetheless, this study shows something more nuanced and interesting. First, more directly, it helps inform the future
design and evaluation of such technologies by means of validating human preferences towards the outputs of mitigation
techniques across multiple sociodemographic dimensions and experimental conditions. In addition, it unveils relevant
evaluation metrics for their training and evaluation. Second, in a meta-analysis, this paper has some critical implications
to the ways in which we attempt to bridge the gap between human-AI studies and the more traditional quantitative
evaluations carried out by the AI community, such as, benchmark-based, data-test-based evaluations. It offers some key
insights relative to the nuances of human considerations when evaluating such systems, which in turn puts into question
how the AI community carries out language model evaluations based on a somewhat flattened view of technology
efficiency and effectiveness.
Pertaining to informing the development and evaluation of mitigation techniques for generative AI, this work
provides key, concrete recommendations, such as, removing harmful content while at the same time preserving key
information, and providing objective and factual responses. When we control the analyses for the independent variables,
the results revealed that participants’ background, namely, native language, AI work experience, and annotation
familiarity significantly affected their preferences and evaluations. This underscores the importance of considering
demographics, especially native-language, and experiential factors in designing and developing content-based mitigation
techniques.
While this might look like as unsurprising, it offers some important insights into the ways humans carry out language-
based evaluations, which in turn offers important insights into human expectations pertaining to the capabilities and
performance of these techniques. Significantly, these techniques make use of relatively small models, in today’s terms,
ranging from 2B up to 8B parameters, or LoRA adapters [19], which are not endowed with the generative capabilities of
larger language models (in the range of 20B to 450B parameters). Hence, this may suggest that one should consider
developing such mitigation techniques for specific socio-demographic contexts (or even, socio-cultural ones).
While it is important to evaluate the performance of mitigating techniques, other metrics, such as, completeness, tone,
selective mitigation, and coherence, became manifest aspects of human evaluation. In our research, we are incorporating
this set of metrics into the ways in which we holistically evaluate the techniques we are developing. The description
and discussion pertaining to this emerging methodology is beyond the scope of this paper, but it suffices to say that
we are carefully investigating the various which in which these metrics are understood and considered by humans,
and how we can “translate” into system-based evaluation metrics. Moreover, we are exploring the ways in which the
introduction of such metrics in turn affect the performance of these techniques.
Today, most of the computational (or automated) evaluation methodologies rely on one or another semantic similarity
or proximity technique (such as, BLEU [36] and its variations, embedding cosine, and the like) that measure the distance
between the outputs of a language model and a particular golden standard (be it humanly curated or synthetically
generated). Increasingly, the use of machine “judges” that automatically score the responses or select a preferred one
(e.g., reward models, or LLM-as-a-Judge) have become also a critical part of evaluating these large-language models. Our
study shows, nonetheless, that contrary to this more flattened approaches for semantic analyses, participants showed a
high sensitivity to linguistic and contextual nuances of the language, for example, by penalizing minor grammar errors
while rewarding preserved semantic contexts. They also tended to evaluate mitigated responses more critically when

8 Limitations
Although we had 57 participants who completed the survey and 17 who were interviewed, our study included a limited
number of participants in certain conditions. It is important to note that our approach differs from the crowdsourcing
methods commonly used in AI evaluations. We emphasize that crowdsourcing often imposes limitations and does not
always ensure representative benefits [8]. Our primary objective was to explore participants’ perceptions of mitigator’s
performance and to examine the complexities involved in evaluating value alignment. We employed several statistical
methods suitable for small samples, and we recognize that having more participants in specific conditions could yield
different results.
9 Conclusion
This paper presents a study that investigate how human participants and evaluate AI-generated contents. In the near
future, the AI and Human-AI communities will be more and more faced with the challenges and opportunities derived
from studies that attempt to reconcile hybrid (human and AI-based) participants. Our research shows a path by which we
can take important, often nuanced, insights from human-studies into the evaluation of large-scale AI-technologies. We
believe that the use of mixed-methods are critical for enabling that. As generative AI becomes a prominent component
of everyday human-computer interactions, we see the need for a greater dialogue among AI, Human-AI, and HCI
researchers to enable a greater exchange of research methods, methodologies, and experiences.
