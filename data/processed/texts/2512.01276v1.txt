Abstract
The standard definition of PAC learning (Valiant 1984) requires learners to succeed under
all distributions—even ones that are intractable to sample from. This stands in contrast to
samplable PAC learning (Blum, Furst, Kearns, and Lipton 1993), where learners only have to
succeed under samplable distributions. We study this distinction and show that samplable PAC
substantially expands the power of efficient learners.
We first construct a concept class that requires exponential sample complexity in standard
PAC but is learnable with polynomial sample complexity in samplable PAC. We then lift this
statistical separation to the computational setting and obtain a separation relative to a random
oracle. Our proofs center around a new complexity primitive, explicit evasive sets , that we
introduce and study. These are sets for which membership is easy to determine but are extremely
hard to sample from.
Our results extend to the online setting to similarly show how its landscape changes when
the adversary is assumed to be efficient instead of computationally unbounded.

1 Introduction
In the PAC model [ Val84], the learner is given labeled data ( x, f(x)) where x is drawn from a
distribution D. The functions f is promised to belong to a known, often simple, concept class,
but no assumptions are made about D. Notably, the learner is required to succeed even under
distributions that are intractable to sample from. These are distributions D for which any generator
G such that G(Unif) = D must have superpolynomial circuit size. Consequently, making even a
single draw x ∼ Dtakes superpolynomial time.
This is arguably an overly stringent requirement. PAC learners are expected to be efficient
and yet the distribution, which can be viewed as an adversary in this context, is allowed to be
computationally unbounded. This stacks the odds in favor of the adversary and is one reason
why efficient PAC learning algorithms have been hard to come by even for simple concept classes.
Furthermore, if D is intractable to sample from then it alone renders the entire learning process
inefficient, regardless of the efficiency of the learner. If the PAC model is to capture efficient
end-to-end learning, from data collection to hypothesis generation, we may as well consider only
samplable distributions. Relatedly, if one believes in the strong Church-Turing thesis, then all
distributions occurring in practice are samplable. Quoting [ Imp95], “Presumably, real life is not so
adversarial that it would solve intractable problems just to give us a hard time.”
Samplable PAC. It is therefore natural to consider a variant of the PAC model, samplable PAC,
where the distribution is assumed to be samplable but is otherwise still unknown and arbitrary. This
strikes the balance of imposing just enough structure on the distribution to place the learner and
adversary on equal footing, while still allowing for enough expressivity to capture the complexities
of real-world learning.
The samplable PAC model was first considered by Blum, Furst, Kearns, and Lipton [ BFKL93].
The focus of their paper was not on the distinction between samplable and standard PAC. Rather,
they showed that efficient learning in samplable PAC is tightly connected to the existence of
fundamental cryptographic primitives. As their main result, they proved that if one-way functions
do not exist, then every concept class is average-case learnable in samplable PAC.1
1.1 This work
We study the distinction between samplable and standard PAC. We are interested in formalizing
the extent to which the assumption of samplability—a seemingly mild and reasonable assumption—
expands the power of efficient learners. In measuring efficiency, we focus on the two most basic
resources in learning: samples and runtime.
Statistical separation. The sample complexity of learning in standard PAC is fairly well-
understood, in large part due to an elegant characterization in terms of VC dimension [ VC71,
BEHW89]—a foundational result now called “The Fundamental Theorem of PAC Learning” [SSBD14].
A VC dimension lower bound is generally viewed as an information-theoretic no-go in terms of
efficient learnability: If a learning task cannot be learned with a reasonable number of samples, that
trivially implies that it cannot be learned in a reasonable amount of time either.

1
Our first result is as follows:
Theorem 1 (See Theorem 5 for the formal version). There is a concept class with exponential
VC dimension—and hence requires exponential sample complexity in standard PAC—but is
learnable with polynomial sample complexity, and in fact even in polynomial time, in samplable
PAC.
This shows that VC dimension lower bounds can be overly pessimistic. A learning task with
large VC dimension may nevertheless be efficiently learnable if one assumes that these samples are
generated according to a reasonable distribution. Put differently, the VC dimension lower bound
may be witnessed by an extremely complicated shattering set, one that is intractable to sample
from and hence arguably will not arise in the real world—indeed, this intuition is the starting point
for our proof of Theorem 1.
Computational separation. The concept class in Theorem 1 has exponential circuit complexity,
necessarily so since the class of size- s circuits has VC dimension ˜O(s). It is natural to then ask
about samplable vs. standard PAC learning of classes of polynomial-size circuits. Such classes are
of interest because functions that arise in practice can be assumed to be efficiently computable, and
relatedly, the barriers to their efficient learnability are solely computational in nature and cannot
rely on information-theoretic impossibility.
As already observed in [ Val84], if RP = NP then every concept class of polynomial-size circuits
is efficiently learnable in polynomial time in standard PAC. So short of proving RP ̸= NP, any
computational separation will have to either rely on complexity assumptions or be relativized.
Implicit in the work of Xiao [ Xia10] is a separation relative to a specific oracle. We discuss [ Xia10]’s
result in Section 3, mentioning for now that this is an oracle relative to which one-way functions do
not exist. This therefore should not be viewed as evidence as to whether such a separation exists in
the unrelativized world—and [Xia10] did not claim it as such—since presumably we believe that
one-way functions do exist in the unrelativized world.
Our second result gives a computational separation conditioned on two complexity assumptions,
a standard one (ironically, the existence of one-way functions) and a new one that we introduce
(the existence of explicit “evasive sets”, Conjecture 2.6):
Theorem 2 (See Theorem 7 for the formal version) . Assume the existence of one-way
functions and explicit evasive sets. There is a concept class of polynomial-size circuits that
requires superpolynomial time to learn in standard PAC, but is learnable in polynomial time
in samplable PAC.
The definition of an explicit evasive set is rather technical and we defer it to Section 2. For now,
we mention that it is a set H ⊆ {0, 1}n that is explicit in the sense that membership in H can be
easily verified (i.e. the function x 7→ 1[x ∈ H] is computable in polynomial time), and yet is evasive
in the sense that any samplable distribution must “mostly miss” it. The crux of the connection to
learning lies in pinning down the appropriate notion of “mostly miss” (Definition 2.2).
2
Random oracle separation. Proving the existence of explicit evasive sets unconditionally is
likely difficult: Like in the case of one-way functions, doing so will imply P ̸= NP (Claim 8.3).
We nevertheless prove that they exist relative to a random oracle (Theorem 8). Since one-way
functions also exist relative to a random oracle, we obtain as a corollary a computational separation
of samplable PAC from standard PAC that holds relative to a random oracle, improving on [Xia10]’s
separation for a specific oracle:
Corollary 1.1 (See Theorem 9 for the formal version) . The assumptions of Theorem 2, and hence
the separation between samplable and standard PAC, hold relative to a random oracle.
As is standard in complexity theory, we view Corollary 1.1 as saying that the speedups offered
by samplable PAC over standard PAC hold not just for certain structured instances (that may
have been tailored for such a separation), but even for generic, unstructured ones. See [ AA14] for a
discussion of this point and the role of random oracle separations more generally.
Remark 1.2 (Evasive sets and uniform generation). The existence of explicit evasive sets is related
to, but differs from, the hardness of uniform generation [JVV86]. In uniform generation an algorithm
is given the description of a circuit C : {0, 1}n → {0, 1} and is asked to sample Unif(C−1(1)) (either
exactly or approximately). The existence of explicit evasive sets implies the hardness of uniform
generation but not vice versa: It could be the case that every polynomial-size circuit C does have
a corresponding polynomial-size circuit that generates Unif(C−1(1)), but such generators are just
hard to construct efficiently.
1.2 Extensions
Separations within samplable PAC. The techniques we use to prove Theorems 1 and 2 extend
to give finer-grained separations within samplable PAC, showing that learning under distributions
with size-s generators can be much easier than under those with size- S generators, even if s is only
slightly smaller than S.
Theorem 3 (See Theorem 6 for the formal version) . For every s ≥ n there is a concept class that
is learnable with polynomial sample complexity under distributions with size- s generators, and yet
requires exponential sample complexity under those with size- S generators for S ≥ Ω(sn log s).
While Theorem 1 shows that there are learning tasks whose sample complexity scale smoothly
with the complexity of the distribution, Theorem 3 shows that there are ones for which a slight
increase in the complexity of the distribution results in a dramatic increase in sample complexity.
See Figure 1. We also prove a computational analogue of Theorem 3. See Theorem 10.

2 Technical overview
Our proofs center around a new notion of evasive sets that we introduce and study. These are sets
that, as their name suggests, evade all samplable distributions. Let us now make this precise.
2.1 Defining evasive sets
Given a distribution D and a set H, both over {0, 1}n, we say that D ε-misses H if it places less
than ε mass on H:
Definition 2.1 (ε-miss). A distribution D ε-misses a set H if D(H) := Prx∼D[x ∈ H] < ε.
Otherwise, we say that D ε-hits H.
A first attempt at the definition of an evasive set H is one for which all samplable distributions
ε-miss it. However, no such H can exist. For any H, a size-O(n) circuit can memorize a specific
x ∈ H and generate the distribution that places all its mass on x. This distribution 1-hits H. More
generally, a size-s circuit can memorize ≈ s/n many points in H. We therefore modify Definition 2.1
to exclude the heaviest elements of the distribution:
Definition 2.2 ((ε, k)-miss). A distribution D (ε, k)-misses H if there exists a set H∗ of size
k such that D(H \ H∗) < ε. Otherwise, we say that D (ε, k)-hits H.

as many points in H as its size allows and output the uniform distribution over those points. If
|H| ≫s, as will be the case in our constructions, this is a very bad approximation of Unif( H).
2.2 A conjecture about explicit evasive sets
A non-explicit construction. Our statistical separation of samplable PAC from standard PAC
(Theorem 1) relies on the existence of large evasive sets. Largeness will be useful for our lower bounds
against standard PAC whereas the evasiveness will be useful for our upper bounds in samplable
PAC. We prove:
5
Lemma 2.5 (Existence of an evasive set) . For any δ ≥ 2−n there is a δ-dense set H ⊆ {0, 1}n that
(ε, O((s log s)/ε))-evades all size- s distributions for all s ≥ n and ε ≥ 4δ.
We prove Lemma 2.5 using the probabilistic method. For intuition, consider the special case of
flat distributions (those that are uniform over their support). For such distributions D, if supp(D)
is sufficiently large, we can show that D is highly unlikely to ε-hit a randomly chosen H. The
failure probability decays exponentially with |supp(D)|, allowing us to union bound over all size- s
distributions with sufficiently large supports. This argument no longer works if supp(D) is small,
but in this case we can use the fact that D trivially (0, |supp(D)|)-misses every H. The parameters
of Lemma 2.5 are near-optimal, since every H is (1 − λ, Θ(λs/n))-hit by any size-s distribution that
is uniform on Θ( s/n) many memorized points in H.
Explicitness. Our computational separation of samplable PAC from standard PAC (Theorem 2)
relies on the existence of a large set that not only evasive, but is furthermore explicit in the sense
that membership in it (i.e. the function x 7→ 1[x ∈ H]) is easy to decide:
Conjecture 2.6 (See Conjecture 8.1 for the formal version) . There is a set H ⊆ {0, 1}n
satisfying:
1. Explicit: Membership in H is computed by a polynomial-size circuit.
2. Large: H has superpolynomial size.
3. Evasive: H (ε, k)-evades all size- s distributions for all s = poly(n) and ε = 1/poly(n),
where k ≤ poly(s, 1/ε).
We study Conjecture 2.6 in detail in Section 8. We show that it implies P ̸= NP (Claim 8.3)
and that it holds relative to a random oracle (Theorem 8). The proof of the latter strengthens that
of Lemma 2.5. The key idea is to show that a samplable distribution D is highly unlikely to hit a
randomly chosen H, even if the circuit generating D is allowed unit-time membership queries to H.

Now consider AH where A is the class of all functions f : {0, 1}n → {0, 1}. It is easy to check

particular, if H has exponential size then learning AH in standard PAC requires exponential sample
complexity. (This is why we are concerned with evasive sets of large size in Lemma 2.5.)
On the other hand, we are able to exploit the evasiveness of H to design an efficient algorithm
for learning AH in samplable PAC:
Lemma 2.7 (Evasiveness implies efficient learners). Let H ⊆ {0, 1}n be a set that (ε, k)-evades
size-s distributions. Then for any concept class C there is an algorithm for learning CH to error
O(ε) under all size- s distributions using O(k/ε) samples and running in time O(nk/ε).
The intuition for Lemma 2.7 is simple. If H is (ε, k)-evasive, then a learner that memorizes the
labels for the k heaviest points in H will only incur ε error. While a learner may not see the k
heaviest points, or even know when it has seen them, we show that memorizing O(k/ε) samples
suffices to achieve good accuracy.
Proof overview of Theorem 2. In the proof of Theorem 1, since H is not explicit and A is the
class of all functions, there are no nontrivial upper bounds on circuit complexity of the functions
in AH. We now describe how we extend the proof strategy so that the separating concept class is a
class of polynomial-size circuits. As mentioned, the lower bounds against standard PAC will now
be computational in nature and can no longer rely on the information-theoretic arguments that
underlie VC dimension lower bounds.
Consider the class FH where H is an explicit evasive set given by Conjecture 2.6 and F is
a pseudorandom function family [ GGM86], the existence of which follows from the existence of
one-way functions. First note that FH now does in fact have polynomial circuit complexity: every
function in this class can be computed by a circuit of size O(S1 + S2), where S1 is the circuit
complexity of deciding membership in H and S2 is the circuit complexity of F.
It is well known that pseudorandom function families are hard to learn: an efficient learner
for F in standard PAC can be used to break F’s security guarantees [ Val84]. We extend this to
show that as long as H is sufficiently large, an efficient learner for FH suffices to break F’s security
guarantees. See Lemma 8.8. Stated in the contrapositive, F’s security guarantees implies hardness
of learning FH in standard PAC. (This is why we are concerned with evasive sets of large size
in Conjecture 2.6.)
3 Related work
Xiao’s separation. As mentioned, a separation between samplable PAC and standard PAC for a
specific oracle is implicit in the work of Xiao:
Theorem 4 (Follows from Theorem 1.3 of [Xia10]) . There is an oracle O such that:
(i) There is a polynomial-time algorithm A such that AO learns SIZEO(n2) in samplable PAC.
(ii) Any algorithm A such that AO learns SIZEO(n2) in standard PAC must take superpolynomial
time.
An inspection of [ Xia10]’s proof shows that O is an oracle relative to which one-way functions

therefore does not shed much light on the relationship between samplable and standard PAC in the

There are several works giving learning algorithms that work in samplable PAC, and its
relaxations, but are not known to work in standard PAC (see e.g. [ BCK+22, HN22, GK23, Kar24]
for a few recent examples).
Distribution-specific learning. Given the apparent difficulty of designing efficient algorithms
in standard PAC, there has been a large body of work on distribution-specific learning. Here the
data is promised to be drawn from a specific distribution, e.g. the uniform distribution. The main
downside is that this is a stylized assumption that limits the practical relevance of the model: We
want our algorithms to succeed for as broad a class of distributions as possible, not just a specific
one. In the case of the uniform distribution in particular, it does not capture much of the richness
real-world distributions that stem from correlations among features.
Samplable PAC can be viewed as a middle ground that simultaneously corrects for the overly
stringent requirements of standard PAC and the overly strong assumptions of distribution-specific
PAC.
Lifting uniform-distribution learners. In the same spirit of bridging this gap between standard
PAC and distribution-specific PAC, recent works [BLMT23, BLST25] show how uniform-distribution
PAC learners can be generically “lifted” to also succeed under various non-uniform yet still structured
classes of distributions.
Compared to these works, our work attempts to bridge the gap “from the opposite direction”.
While these lifters scale up the distribution-specific model, the samplable PAC model scales down
the distribution-free model (i.e. standard PAC).
Computable PAC and online learning. Another recent line of work [AABD +20, AABD+21,
Ste22, DRKRS23] studies the distinction between standard PAC and a variant known as computable
PAC where learners are restricted to be computable. Among other results, these works show
that there are classes with finite VC dimension that are not learnable in computable PAC. See
also [HBD23, DRKS25] for the online analogue.
The focus of our work is on statistical and computational complexity in settings where com-
putability is not an issue, rather than the distinction between computable and uncomputable
learners.

4 Discussion and future work
Samplability is generally viewed as a baseline requirement for real-world distributions, not a
characterization. See Figure 3. Samplable PAC is therefore only a first cut at refining the standard
PAC model.
We see two takeaways from this. First, as alluded to in the introduction, lower bounds in
standard PAC—be they statistical or computational—may be overly pessimistic: While certain
learning tasks may have hard instances, these hard instances themselves may be hard to find, and
arguably will not occur in practice. Second, this calls for a better understanding of the actual
structure of real-world distributions beyond just samplability, which can then be leveraged in the
design of learning algorithms. This falls within the overall agenda of going beyond the worst-case
analysis of algorithms [ Rou21]. Our techniques suggest the possibility of deeper connections to the
complexity of sampling and uniform generation.
A concrete open problem is that of characterizing the sample complexity of learning in sam-
plable PAC. Sample complexity in standard PAC is characterized by VC dimension—what is the
corresponding characterization for samplable PAC? More generally, is there a characterization that
takes both function and distribution complexity into account?

5 Preliminaries
Basic notation and writing conventions. We write [n] to denote the set {1, 2, . . . , n}. The
length of a bitstring x ∈ {0, 1}n is |x|. Boldface letters, e.g. x, H, denote random variables. We
write Unif(H) to denote the uniform distribution over the set H and Ber(δ) to denote the Bernoulli
distribution with mean δ. For H ⊆ {0, 1}n, the density of H is |H|/2n. If |H|/2n ≥ δ, we say that
H is δ-dense. We write H = {0, 1}n \ H to denote the complement of H. Given a distribution D
over {0, 1}n and a point x ∈ {0, 1}n, we let D(x) := Pry∼D[x = y]. Similarly, for a set H ⊆ {0, 1}n,
we let D(H) := Prx∼D[x ∈ H].
We use two basic asymptotic conventions: We say a function σ(n) is superpolynomial if for every

5.3 Learning theory
Definition 5.6 (PAC learning [Val84]). For any concept class C, we say an algorithm A learns
C to error ε over distribution D using m samples if the following holds: For any f ∈ C, given
m independent samples of the form (x, f(x)) where x ∼ D, A returns a hypothesis h, that with
probability at least 2/3, satisfies Prx∼D[f(x) ̸= h(x)] ≤ ε.
We say that A learns C to error ε using m samples if for every distribution D, A learns C to
error ε over the distribution D using m samples.
Definition 5.7 (VC dimension [VC71]). Let C be a concept class consisting of Boolean functions.
A set S ⊆ {0, 1}n is shattered if for every labeling ℓ : S → {0, 1}, there is an f ∈ Csuch that
f(x) = ℓ(x) for every x ∈ S. The VC dimension of C, VCdim(C), is the size of the largest set which
is shattered.
We will use the following fundamental result in learning theory which constructs a distribution
over a shattering set and shows that learning over this distribution requires many samples.
Fact 5.8 (Hard distribution over shattering set [ BEHW89, EHKV89]). Let S = {x(1), . . . , x(d)} be
a shattering set for a concept class C. For any ε ≤ 1/8, let D be the distribution defined by

8 Conjecture 2.6 and the proofs of computational separations
We introduce the hardness assumption that there exists explicit evasive sets:
Conjecture 8.1 (Formal version of Conjecture 2.6) . There exists sets {Hn ⊆ {0, 1}n}n∈N and
constant c satisfying the following.
1. Explicit: Membership in Hn is computed by a circuit of size poly(n).
2. Large: Hn contains superpolynomially many points.
3. Evasive: For all s = poly(n), Hn (1/s, sc)-evades all size- s distributions.
As far as we know, Conjecture 8.1 could hold with stronger parameters. For example, if the best
strategy for sampling H is to memorize ≈ s/n elements of H, then Conjecture 8.1 holds even with
c = 1.
We use Conjecture 8.1 in the formal version of Theorem 2.
Theorem 7 (Formal version of Theorem 2). If Conjecture 8.1 is true and one-way functions exist,
there is a concept class C with the following properties.
(i) C is a subclass of polynomial-size circuits.
(ii) C is easy to learn in samplable PAC: For every s(n) ≤ poly(n) and ε(n) ≥ 1/poly(n), there is
a polytime algorithm that learns C to accuracy (1 − ε(n)) on size-s(n) distributions.
23
(iii) C is hard to learn in standard PAC: There is a distribution under which learning C to constant
accuracy requires superpolynomial time.
Remark 8.2 (The quantitative time lower bound in standard PAC) . Theorem 7 obtains “only”
a superpolynomial time lower bound in standard PAC. This stems from only assuming one-way
functions that defeat all polynomial time adversaries. With stronger assumptions, this time lower
bound could be easily strengthened to 2 Ω(n). For that version, we would need to assume one-way
functions for which, all time O(2cn) adversaries can only achieve advantage 2 −cn for some constant
c. We would also need to slightly strengthen Conjecture 8.1 to hold for larger sets, Hn containing
2cn points. In this section, we just prove the superpolynomial time lower bound using the more
standard cryptographic assumption as the exponential time lower bound has essentially the same
proof.

1. In Section 8.1, we show that it is likely difficult to unconditionally prove Conjecture 8.1 by
showing that doing so requires separating P from NP.
2. In Section 8.2, we provide formal evidence in favor of Conjecture 8.1, showing that it is true
relative to a random oracle.
3. In Section 8.3, we prove Theorem 7.
4. In Section 8.4, we sketch the proof of Theorem 10, the computational analogue of Theorem 3.
Its proof is essentially the same as Theorem 7.
