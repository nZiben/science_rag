MAINTENANCE

Abstract.Predictive maintenance in manufacturing environments presents a challenging opti-
mization problem characterized by extreme cost asymmetry, where missed failures incur costs
roughly fifty times higher than false alarms. Conventional machine learning approaches typically
optimize statistical accuracy metrics that do not reflect this operational reality and cannot reliably
distinguish causal relationships from spurious correlations. This study evaluates eight predictive
models, ranging from baseline statistical approaches to formal causal inference methods, on a dataset
of 10,000 CNC machines with a 3.3 percent failure prevalence. The formal causal inference model
(L5) achieved estimated annual cost savings of 1.16 million USD (a 70.2 percent reduction), outper-
forming the best correlation-based decision tree model (L3) by approximately 80,000 USD per year.
The causal model matched the highest observed recall (87.9 percent) while reducing false alarms by
97 percent (from 165 to 5) and attained a precision of 92.1 percent, with a train-test performance
gap of only 2.6 percentage points. These results indicate that causal AI methods, when combined
with domain knowledge, can yield superior financial outcomes and more interpretable predictions
compared to correlation-based approaches in predictive maintenance applications.

2.Introduction
Manufacturing faces a maintenance dilemma. Reactive maintenance leads to costly downtime,
while overly cautious preventive maintenance wastes resources on healthy equipment. The core
challenge is an extreme cost asymmetry across industrial equipment: a missed failure can cost
tens of thousands of dollars (unplanned downtime, emergency repairs, production losses), while a
false alarm costs only hundreds of dollars (scheduled inspection, standard parts). For the CNC
machines analyzed in this study, we use representative costs of 25,000 USD for missed failures and
500 USD for false alarms, reflecting typical manufacturing industry values where the cost ratio
between unplanned downtime and preventive maintenance ranges from 20:1 to 100:1 depending on
equipment criticality. Table 1 summarizes the four possible prediction outcomes, their associated
costs, and business impact.
Outcome Description Cost Business Impact
True Positive (TP)Correctly predict failure 5,000 USD Scheduled repair prevents downtime
False Positive (FP)Wrongly predict failure 500 USD Wasted inspection/parts
False Negative (FN)Miss actual failure 25,000 USD Emergency downtime + repairs
True Negative (TN)Correctly predict no failure 0 USD Normal production

typical manufacturing industry values where unplanned downtime incurs significant
production losses, emergency labor, and expedited parts costs, while preventive
maintenance requires only scheduled labor and standard parts procurement [8].
Standard machine learning models optimize for statistical accuracy metrics such as accuracy,
precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC, which either ignore class imbalance or treat all predic-
tion errors equally. Accuracy is misleading in imbalanced datasets—a model that always predicts
“no failure” achieves 96.7% accuracy on our data while providing zero business value. Precision
focuses on minimizing false alarms but may miss critical failures, while recall prioritizes catching
all failures but may trigger hundreds of unnecessary inspections. F1-score balances precision and
recall through their harmonic mean, and AUC-ROC evaluates the model’s ranking ability across
all possible thresholds, but neither metric accounts for the actual business costs of different error
types. All these metrics fail to capture the 50:1 cost ratio between false negatives and false positives
that is typical for manufacturing equipment, where unplanned downtime costs far exceed scheduled
2
maintenance [8]. Furthermore, correlation-based models identify patterns that co-occur with fail-
ures but cannot distinguish causation from spurious relationships, leading to poor generalizability
and a lack of actionable insights.
Our goal is to determine if Causal AI can deliver superior business value over state-of-the-art,
cost-optimized correlation-based ML in a real-world predictive maintenance scenario.

3.2.2.Correlation-Based Models (L0-L3).We developed four correlation-based models of increasing
sophistication, representing the spectrum of traditional machine learning techniques applied to
predictive maintenance.
Model L0: No-Skill Baseline.The no-skill model serves as the minimal performance floor.
This model predicts “no failure” for every observation, regardless of sensor readings or operating
conditions. While this may seem trivial, it establishes a critical reference point. In highly imbal-
anced datasets like ours (96.7% non-failure cases), a naive model that always predicts the majority
class achieves 96.7% accuracy. This demonstrates why accuracy is a misleading metric for imbal-
anced problems. The no-skill model catches zero failures, misses all 66 failures in the test set, and
incurs the full 1,650,000 USD baseline cost. Any meaningful predictive model must outperform
this baseline.
Model L1: Balanced Logistic Regression.The first predictive model applies logistic regres-
sion, a fundamental statistical classification technique. Logistic regression models the log-odds of
failure as a linear combination of input features: air temperature, process temperature, rotational
speed, torque, tool wear, and one-hot encoded machine type. To address class imbalance [10], we
employ scikit-learn’s [4] class weight=‘balanced’ parameter, which automatically adjusts the loss
function to penalize misclassification of the minority class (failures) more heavily than the majority
class (non-failures).
This approach uses the default decision threshold of 0.5 probability–if the model predicts a failure
probability above 50%, it classifies the observation as a failure. Features are standardized to zero
mean and unit variance to ensure numerical stability and proper weight scaling. The model is
trained on 8,000 machines and evaluated on the held-out test set of 2,000 machines.
Model L2: Cost-Aware Logistic Regression.WhileModel L1addresses class imbalance,
it still treats all prediction errors equally.Model L2enhances the logistic regression approach by
optimizing the decision threshold explicitly for business costs rather than statistical balance [7].
After training the same logistic regression model asModel L1, we perform a grid search over possible
probability thresholds from 0.01 to 0.99 to find the optimal threshold to minimize the total annual
cost. For each candidate threshold, we generate predictions on the training set, calculate the
resulting confusion matrix, apply our business cost model (5K USD for TP, 500 USD for FP,
25K USD for FN, 0 USD for TN), and compute total cost. The threshold that minimizes total
training-set cost is selected as the final decision boundary. This cost-aware optimization explicitly
encodes the 50-to-1 cost asymmetry between false negatives and false positives, shifting the decision
boundary to favor higher recall even at the expense of increased false alarms.
Note that the threshold is learned on the training set and treated as a learned hyperparameter for
test set evaluation.
Model L3: Cost-Aware Decision Tree.While logistic regression models linear relationships,
many real-world failure mechanisms involve complex feature interactions. A heat dissipation failure,
for example, may only occur when the temperature differential is low AND rotational speed is slow
simultaneously - a conjunction that linear models struggle to capture.
Model L3employs a decision tree classifier to learn non-linear decision boundaries through re-
cursive feature splits. Decision trees naturally handle feature interactions by creating hierarchical
rules: “If temperature differential<8.6K, then if rotational speed<1380 RPM, predict failure.”
To prevent overfitting, we constrain the tree with hyperparameters: maximum depth of 5 levels,
minimum 50 samples required to split a node, and minimum 20 samples required in each leaf node.
After training this regularized decision tree, we apply the same cost-aware threshold optimization
asModel L2. For each probability threshold (decision trees can output class probabilities), we
compute total business cost on the training set and select the threshold that minimizes cost. This
combination of non-linear modeling and business-aligned optimization represents the state-of-the-
art for correlation-based approaches.

3.2.3.Probabilistic and Causal Approaches.To explore whether fundamentally different modeling
paradigms can improve upon correlation-based methods, we developed two advanced models incor-
porating uncertainty quantification and formal causal inference.
Model L4: Bayesian Logistic Regression.While standard logistic regression produces point
estimates of failure probability, Bayesian approaches quantify uncertainty in those estimates.Model
L4implements Bayesian logistic regression using PyMC [5], a probabilistic programming framework.
The model architecture mirrorsModel L1’s logistic regression structure but replaces point-
estimated parameters with probability distributions. Each feature weight is assigned a Normal
prior distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 10, expressing weak prior belief that fea-
tures have moderate effects. The intercept term receives a Normal(0, 10) prior. Rather than finding
a single “best” set of parameters, we use Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to explore
the posterior distribution of parameters given the data.
Specifically, we run the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) with 500 posterior samples per chain, 300
tuning iterations, and 2 parallel chains, targeting an acceptance rate of 0.85. This produces 1,000
posterior samples representing plausible parameter values. For prediction, we average predicted
probabilities across all posterior samples, yielding a probability estimate that incorporates param-
eter uncertainty.
After obtaining Bayesian predictions, we again optimize the decision threshold for business costs.
The key advantage of the Bayesian approach is interpretability: we can quantify uncertainty in
predictions and examine posterior distributions of feature weights to assess which sensors provide
reliable predictive signal.
Model L5: Causal Model.All previous models (L0-L4) learn correlations in observational
data. They identify that certain sensor patterns co-occur with failures but cannot distinguish
causation from correlation.Model L5takes a fundamentally different approach: constructing an
explicit causal model and using formal causal inference methods to identify causal effects.
The causal graph (Figure 1) encodes engineering principles governing CNC machine failure. The
graph captures three physical mechanisms:
Thermal failureoccurs when insufficient temperature differential (process minus ambient air)
prevents heat dissipation, causing component degradation.
Power failureresults when mechanical power (torque×rotational speed) exceeds electrical and
mechanical capacity.
Overstrain failurereflects cumulative stress where tool wear×torque exceeds material limits.
The graph models these derived variables (temperature differential, power, overstrain) as inter-
mediate nodes mediating sensor effects on failure. This structure reflects CNC physics: sensors
combine through physical laws to create stress conditions exceeding thresholds, making causal
relationships invariant to operational changes.
The methodology follows a five-step causal inference pipeline
1) Encode domain knowledge as a DAG (Figure 1) with nodes (sensor measurements, machine
type, failure) and edges (causal relationships).
2) Define treatment variables: tool wear (replacement timing), torque (operational parame-
ters), and rotational speed (machine settings). Temperature variables are outcomes, not
treatments.
3) Apply Pearl’s do-calculus [2] to determine causal effect identifiability. The backdoor crite-
rion [3] identifies adjustment sets blocking confounding paths.
4) Estimate causal effects by fitting linear regressions with identified adjustment sets, obtaining
unbiased causal coefficients.
5) Create derived causal features: temperature differential (thermal stress), power (energy dis-
sipation), and overstrain (cumulative mechanical stress) with clear physical interpretations.

Finally, we train a Gradient Boosting Classifier [12] on both the original sensor features and
the derived causal features. Gradient boosting builds an ensemble of weak learners (decision trees)
that collectively capture complex non-linear relationships. As with previous models, we optimize
the decision threshold for business costs.
The critical advantage of this causal approach is that it provides both predictive performance
and interpretability. When the model predicts a failure, we can trace the prediction back to specific
causal pathways—overstrain exceeding safe limits, thermal stress from inadequate cooling, or power
demand exceeding capacity. This transparency enables targeted interventions rather than generic
maintenance.
Model L6: Rule-Based Causal Model.We also implement a rule set that mirrors the engi-
neering heuristics described in the dataset documentation: failures are declared when any causal
7
mechanism (tool wear band, insufficient heat dissipation, unsafe power level, or torque-driven over-
strain) breaches its learned threshold. Random search tunes those thresholds to minimize cost.
The approach provides perfect recall, but, as discussed later, it generates hundreds of precaution-
ary inspections and is therefore best interpreted as an upper bound on achievable recall rather than
a deployable policy.
Model L7: PyMC BART.Finally, we train a Bayesian Additive Regression Tree (BART)
classifier using the PyMC-BART implementation [14][5]. BART is a non-parametric ensemble that
automatically discovers non-linear combinations of features while delivering posterior uncertainty
for every prediction. We feed BART the same causal feature set as L5 to test whether a flexible
Bayesian learner can match causal performance without the explicit do-calculus pipeline. When
we also let BART consume raw sensor readings and automatically generated interaction terms, it
rediscovers the same latent constructs (temperature differential, power, overstrain), but the absence
of structural constraints increases false positives by 2−3X. This sensitivity analysis confirms the
intuition that automatic feature generation alone can approximate causal features, and yet graph-
informed regularization is still required to keep operational costs low.

3.2.4.Validation Strategy.To ensure results are robust and not artifacts of a single train-test split,
we implemented a comprehensive validation strategy. All models (L0 through L5) were trained and
evaluated across five different random seeds (42, 43, 44, 45, and 46). For each seed, the dataset is
randomly shuffled before the 80-20 stratified split, producing different training and test sets while
maintaining the 3.3% failure rate in both partitions.
Performance metrics are computed independently for each seed, then averaged to produce final
results. This multi-seed validation ensures that observed performance differences reflect genuine
model capabilities rather than fortunate (or unfortunate) data partitioning. If a model performs well
on one seed but poorly on others, this indicates instability and overfitting. Conversely, consistent
performance across seeds demonstrates robust generalization.
Additionally, we examine the generalization gap; the difference between training-set performance
and test-set performance. A large gap indicates overfitting: the model has memorized training data
patterns that do not generalize. We report both training and test savings percentages, with the
gap measured in percentage points.

4.1.Detailed Model Analysis. L0: No-Skill Baseline.As expected, the no-skill model does
not contribute anything. By always predicting “no failure,” it achieves the naive accuracy of 96.7%
(equal to the base rate of non-failures) while catching zero actual failures. All 66 failures in the
test set occur unexpectedly, each incurring the full 25,000 USD emergency cost, totaling 1,650,000
USD. This model serves only as the reference point against which all other approaches must be
measured.
L1: Balanced Logistic Regression.The first predictive model demonstrates that even simple
linear models can extract meaningful signal from sensor data.Model L1catches 54 out of 66 failures
(81.8% recall), preventing 1,350,000 USD in potential downtime costs. These 54 true positives cost
5,000 USD each in preventive maintenance, totaling 270,000 USD.
However, the model also generates 346 false positives—unnecessary maintenance interventions
on healthy machines. At 500 USD each, these false alarms cost 173,000 USD in wasted labor.
The 12 missed failures (false negatives) each incur the full 25,000 USD emergency cost, totaling
300,000 USD. Summing all components yields total cost of 743,000 USD, representing 907,000 USD
in savings compared to reactive maintenance—a 55% cost reduction.
The model’s 13.5% precision indicates that only about 1 in 7 alarms corresponds to a real failure.
While this may seem poor, it is acceptable given the cost structure: each real failure costs 25,000
USD while each false alarm costs only 500 USD. The model can afford 50 false alarms per true
positive and still break even economically.

L2: Cost-Aware Logistic Regression.Model L2applies the same logistic regression ar-
chitecture as L1 but optimizes the decision threshold explicitly for business costs. The threshold
optimization pushes the decision boundary lower, making the model more aggressive in predict-
ing failures. This increases recall slightly to 83.3% (55 out of 66 failures caught) but generates
substantially more false alarms—488 instead of 346.
The cost breakdown reveals why this strategy backfires. The 55 true positives cost 275,000
USD, the 488 false positives cost 244,000 USD, and the 11 false negatives cost 275,000 USD,
totaling 794,000 USD. While the model catches one additional failure compared to L1, it generates
142 additional false alarms, adding 71,000 USD in unnecessary intervention costs. Net savings of
856,000 USD are actually 51,000 USD lower than L1.
This counterintuitive result illustrates a subtle point: threshold optimization on the training set
can lead to overfitting of the decision boundary. The optimal threshold for training data may be too
aggressive for test data if the model’s probability calibration is poor [11].Model L2’s probabilities
are not well-calibrated—many predicted “high risk” machines do not actually fail—so lowering the
threshold excessively inflates false alarms.
L3: Cost-Aware Decision Tree.Model L3represents the best correlation-based approach
by combining non-linear modeling with constrained complexity. The decision tree architecture
naturally captures feature interactions such as “heat dissipation failure occurs when temperature
differential is low AND rotational speed is low.” Regularization hyperparameters (maximum depth
5, minimum 50 samples per split, minimum 20 samples per leaf) prevent the tree from memorizing
training data noise.
This model catches 58 out of 66 failures (87.9% recall), a substantial improvement over L1’s
54. Critically, it does so with only 165 false alarms—less than half of L1’s 346 false positives.
This superior precision (26.0%, meaning about 1 in 4 alarms is real) reflects the model’s ability to
identify genuine failure patterns rather than overfitting to spurious correlations.
The cost breakdown is favorable: 58 true positives at 5,000 USD each cost 290,000 USD, 165
false positives at 500 USD each cost 82,500 USD, and 8 missed failures at 25,000 USD each cost
200,000 USD. Total cost of 572,500 USD yields 1,077,500 USD in annual savings—a 65.3% cost
reduction. This represents the ceiling of what correlation-based machine learning can achieve on
this problem.
L4: Bayesian Logistic Regression.Model L4applies Bayesian inference to quantify predic-
tion uncertainty, but this additional sophistication does not translate to improved test-set perfor-
mance. The model achieves 81.8% recall and 13.5% precision, identical to L1’s results. Total cost
of 878,000 USD yields 772,000 USD in savings—better than reactive maintenance but worse than
simple balanced logistic regression (L1).
The modest performance suggests that parameter uncertainty, while theoretically valuable, does
not substantially improve predictions when the underlying model class (linear logistic regression)
has limited expressiveness. The Bayesian framework provides interpretability—we can examine pos-
terior distributions to assess which features have reliably non-zero effects—but does not overcome
the fundamental limitation that linear models cannot capture the complex non-linear interactions
present in failure mechanisms.
The computational cost of Bayesian inference (several minutes for MCMC sampling versus sec-
onds for standard logistic regression) further reduces practical appeal unless the uncertainty quan-
tification itself is valuable for decision-making. For this predictive maintenance application, action-
able predictions matter more than prediction intervals.
L5: Causal Model.Model L5achieves the highest performance across all metrics, demonstrat-
ing the value of causal reasoning. The model catches 58 out of 66 failures (87.9% recall), matching

Model L3’s recall. However, it does so with only 5 false positives compared to L3’s 165—a dra-
matic 97% reduction in false alarms. Precision of 92.1% means that more than 9 out of 10 alarms

This exceptional precision-recall balance translates to substantial cost savings. The 58 true

The model’s superiority stems from its causal architecture. By encoding domain knowledge in
the causal graph and deriving physically meaningful features (temperature differential for thermal
stress, power for energy dissipation, overstrain for mechanical stress), the model learns relationships
that generalize robustly. When the model predicts a failure, it does so because a specific causal
mechanism has been activated—not because of a spurious correlation in the training data.
For example, a prediction might be traced to high overstrain: tool wear has reached 215 minutes
while torque is 56 Nm, producing an overstrain index of 12,040 that exceeds the safe threshold
of 11,000. This level of interpretability enables targeted interventions: the maintenance team can
replace the tool before reaching the dangerous threshold, addressing the root cause directly.
L6: Rule-Based Causal Model.The threshold-based rule system provides a useful upper
bound on recall. By declaring a failure whenever any mechanism is even close to its safety margin,
the rules catch all 66 deterministic failures (100% recall) and reduce total cost to 477,000 USD.
However, the policy produces 294 false alarms—orders of magnitude more than the five false alarms
produced by L5. In practice that would translate into hundreds of unnecessary stoppages per week.
We therefore treat L6 as a “safety-first” bound that validates the physics of the rules, not as a
candidate deployment policy.
L7: PyMC BART Model.BART delivers 1,109,000 USD in savings (67.2%) with recall
of 89.4%, but its precision drops to 29.4% (142 false positives). This outcome answers potential
questions around automatic feature generation: even a powerful Bayesian ensemble that can dis-
cover complex interactions still benefits from graph-regularized features. Without explicit causal
constraints the model develops conservative decision boundaries, trading cost savings for a larger

4.1.1.Comparative Analysis: Causal vs. Correlation.The performance gap betweenModel L5
(causal) andModel L3(best correlation) quantifies the value of causal reasoning.Model L5saves
an additional 80,000 USD annually (7.4% improvement in savings percentage) while generating 160
fewer false alarms—a 97% reduction in operational disruption. L6 shows that we can squeeze a few
more dollars out of the problem by firing every possible alarm, but at the cost of 294 inspections;
L5 reaches nearly the same savings with almost no false positives.
This improvement cannot be attributed to model complexity. L3 is a single, carefully regularized
decision tree rather than a gradient-boosted ensemble, whereas L5 couples do-calculus adjustments
with gradient boosting and L7 adds a Bayesian additive regression tree. When we train powerful
ensembles (gradient boosting and BART) on raw features without causal guidance, they rediscover
similar splits yet inflate the false-positive rate. The difference lies in the features themselves.Model
L3learns from raw sensor readings, discovering correlations like “high torque readings are associated
with failure.”Model L5learns from causally derived features, understanding mechanisms like
“high torque combined with worn tools causes mechanical overstrain failure,” whileModel L7
demonstrates that even a flexible Bayesian ensemble still benefits from causal priors.
When operating conditions shift slightly—perhaps a new batch of machines has different tem-
perature baselines, or a process change alters typical torque ranges—Model L3’s correlations may
degrade, whileModel L5’s causal relationships remain valid. The physics of mechanical overstrain

do not change when ambient temperature drifts, whereas the correlation between specific torque
values and failure does.
This distinction has profound implications for model deployment and longevity.Model L3re-
quires periodic retraining as data distributions shift.Model L5’s causal structure provides inherent
robustness to distribution changes, as causal relationships are invariant properties of the system
rather than contingent patterns in historical data.

5.Conclusion
This study evaluated six predictive maintenance models on a CNC machine failure dataset,
comparing traditional correlation-based machine learning approaches with formal causal inference
methods. Results indicate that the causal AI approach (Model L5) achieved superior performance
on business-relevant metrics, delivering 1,157,500 USD in annual cost savings (70.2% reduction)
while maintaining high recall (87.9%) and exceptional precision (92.1%). The causal model outper-
formed the best correlation-based decision tree (Model L3) by 80,000 USD annually while reducing
false alarms by 97%.
This work demonstrates several important findings for predictive maintenance applications:
12
(1)Business-aligned optimization: Models optimized explicitly for business costs (account-
ing for the 50:1 cost asymmetry between false negatives and false positives) substantially
outperform accuracy-optimized approaches.
(2)Causal superiority: When domain knowledge enables construction of causal graphs, for-
mal causal inference methods can provide both superior predictive performance and inter-
pretable root-cause explanations for predictions.
(3)Bayesian ensembles still benefit from structure: The PyMC BART model validates
that automatic feature generation can approach causal performance, but without graph-
informed constraints, it issues substantially more false positives.
(4)Robust generalization: Causally-derived features demonstrate stronger generalization
(2.6 percentage point train-test gap) compared to raw sensor correlations (4.1 percentage
points for the best correlation model), consistent with theoretical expectations that causal
relationships are invariant under distribution shifts.
(5)Operational benefits: The dramatic reduction in false alarms (97% fewer than correlation-
based approaches) minimizes operational disruption while maintaining safety through high
recall.
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged:
(1)Dataset scope: The analysis uses a single dataset of 10,000 CNC machines. Generalization
to other machine types, manufacturing environments, or operational conditions requires
additional validation.
(2)Causal graph construction: The causal DAG was manually constructed based on domain
knowledge of CNC machine failure mechanisms. In domains where such expertise is un-
available, causal discovery algorithms could be explored, though they introduce additional
challenges.
(3)Cost estimates: The business costs (25,000 USD for failures, 5,000 USD for preventive
maintenance, 500 USD for false alarms) are representative estimates. Actual costs vary by
industry, machine criticality, and operational context.
(4)Static analysis: This study evaluates models on historical data without real-world de-
ployment. Practical implementation would require monitoring for distribution drift, model
degradation, and integration with existing maintenance management systems.
(5)Class imbalance: The 3.3% failure rate, while realistic for well-maintained equipment,
limits the number of positive examples available for model training. Larger datasets with
more failure observations could improve model performance.
Future research directions include: (1) validation across multiple manufacturing domains and
equipment types; (2) investigation of causal discovery algorithms to automate DAG construction;
(3) development of online learning approaches for continual model adaptation; (4) integration with
IoT sensor networks and SCADA systems for real-time deployment; and (5) extension to multi-
component systems with cascading failure modes.
